# Scientific sources and boundaries

Accessed 2026-10-27. Original illustrations and explanatory text; no external imagery copied.

1. Florida State University, Molecular Expressions, Refraction of Light
   https://micro.magnet.fsu.edu/primer/lightandcolor/refractionintro.html
   Supports: submerged objects can appear at a shallower position because rays refract at the interface; visual back-projection along straight paths produces a virtual image, not a moved object.
2. Oregon State University, Refraction
   https://lipa.physics.oregonstate.edu/sec_refraction.html
   Supports: Snell's law and angles measured relative to the normal; water-to-air transmitted rays at oblique incidence bend away from the normal below the critical angle.
3. Florida State University, Science, Optics and You, Refraction of Light
   https://micro.magnet.fsu.edu/optics/lightandcolor/refraction.html
   Gives approximate visible-light indices air 1.0003 and water 1.33. The scene uses air 1 and water 1.333 as an educational approximation.
4. American Museum of Natural History, OLogy, Refraction
   https://www.amnh.org/explore/ology/physics/see-the-light2/refraction
   Context for everyday apparent displacement across a water boundary.

## Scope
- Flat, still water-to-air interface. Container wall refraction, wave optics, dispersion, reflections and scattering are omitted.
- Light propagates from the illuminated coin toward the observer. Dashed backward extensions are construction lines, not light travelling backward.
- The solid coin remains fixed. Any raised dashed or translucent marker is explicitly an apparent/virtual position.
- The apparent position depends on viewing direction. No universal three-quarter-depth ratio is claimed; that familiar approximation is limited to near-normal viewing.
- At normal incidence there is no change in direction. The showcased rays are oblique and below the critical angle.
- Diagrams are educational schematics, not scale or experimental data. Two neighboring rays give a local virtual-image construction; a plane refracting surface does not form one exact image point for arbitrary ray angles.
